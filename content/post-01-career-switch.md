# Why I Left the Oil Fields to Write Code

*And what drilling mud taught me about software*

---

In February 2020, I quit my job as a drilling fluids engineer and enrolled in a coding bootcamp.

My colleagues thought I was having a crisis. My family thought I was throwing away a career. My wife thought it was the best idea I'd ever had.

She was right.

---

## What I was doing before

If you've never heard of a drilling fluids engineer, you're not alone. It's one of those jobs that exists entirely out of public view, doing work that civilization depends on.

My job was to manage the fluid — "drilling mud" — that circulates through an active oil well while it's being drilled. That fluid does about a dozen things simultaneously: it cools the drill bit, carries rock cuttings back to the surface, maintains pressure so the wellbore doesn't collapse, and prevents the kind of blowout that makes national news.

When something went wrong — and things went wrong constantly — it was my problem to fix it. On a rig, there's no ticket system. No sprint planning. No "we'll scope that next quarter." There's a multi-million dollar drill string stuck in the earth and a client losing money by the hour, and everyone is looking at you.

I loved it. And I was good at it.

But after two years in the field, something kept nagging at me.

---

## The thing I couldn't ignore

Every problem I solved on a rig had a ceiling. I'd diagnose the issue, apply the fix, write the report, and move on to the next well. The solution lived in that report, maybe got referenced once or twice, and then faded.

Software felt different. You solve a problem once and it scales infinitely. A good piece of code runs the same whether one person uses it or a million. There was something about that leverage that I couldn't stop thinking about.

I also kept watching the engineers around me spend hours on manual data entry, spreadsheets that broke constantly, reports that took half a day to compile. And I kept thinking — *someone should build a tool for this.* Eventually I realized that someone could be me.

---

## The part nobody tells you about switching careers

I went through Flatiron School in 2020. I'm not going to tell you it was easy or that I immediately "found my passion" or any of the sanitized version of the story.

What actually happened is I spent about six months feeling profoundly stupid. I had spent years becoming genuinely competent at a hard technical field, and suddenly I was a beginner again. That's a specific kind of humbling that's hard to describe if you haven't felt it.

But my background helped in ways I didn't expect. Petroleum engineering trains you to think in systems. Every well is a complex interconnected system where a change in one variable ripples through everything else. That mental model maps onto software architecture better than I could have anticipated.

And the high-stakes debugging? Turns out tracing a production bug through a Rails app isn't so different from diagnosing why a well is losing returns at 8,000 feet. You form a hypothesis, test it, eliminate possibilities, narrow it down. The tools are different. The thinking isn't.

---

## What came next

I got my first job in software in late 2020 — a React Native internship at a small health tech startup in Denver. I built a symptom-checker chatbot. It was a far cry from managing drilling operations on an active rig in the Permian Basin, but I was hooked.

In 2021 I joined Whitelabel Collaborative as a full stack developer. I've been there since — building healthcare portals, real estate platforms, construction tools, and most recently integrating AI agents into production workflows.

Also in 2021, my wife and I sold most of what we owned, bought a travel trailer, and hit the road.

We've been out here ever since — two humans, two cats, one trailer, moving through the US and Canada. I write code somewhere new almost every month. Sometimes that's a campsite in the desert. Sometimes it's a full hookup park in the suburbs. The work is the same either way.

---

## What I'd tell someone considering a switch

Three things:

**Your existing expertise is an asset, not a liability.** The software industry is full of people who only know software. If you deeply understand another field — medicine, law, construction, finance, whatever — you are immediately more valuable in that domain than someone who's only ever written code.

**The imposter syndrome never fully goes away, but it changes shape.** In the beginning it's "I don't know anything." A few years in it's "I don't know enough." That shift is progress, even if it doesn't feel like it.

**The path doesn't have to be linear.** I didn't go from oil fields to FAANG. I went from oil fields to a bootcamp to a startup internship to a small agency doing healthcare software while living in a trailer. It's a weird path. It's my path. It's working.

---

I'm five years into this career now. I still think about the rig sometimes — the noise, the smell of drilling fluid, the particular satisfaction of a well going back on track after a rough night.

But I don't miss it. What I'm building now scales. And for someone who grew up watching solutions disappear into filing cabinets, that still feels a little like magic.
